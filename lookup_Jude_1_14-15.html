Commentary Comparison

Tyndale Open Study Notes · Jamieson-Fausset-Brown Bible Commentary · John Gill's Exposition of the Whole Bible · Matthew Henry's Complete Commentary

Points of Agreement

The Tyndale Open Study Notes emphasizes that Jude identifies himself as "a slave of Jesus Christ" and "brother of James," acknowledging both his submission to Christ's lordship and his familial connection to a prominent church leader. All four commentaries agree on this basic identification, with Jamieson-Fausset-Brown noting that Jude "calls himself a servant of Jesus Christ, esteeming that a most honourable title," while Gill explains the name means "praise" and Matthew Henry observes that being Christ's servant is "more honourable...than to be akin to him according to the flesh."

There is universal agreement on the nature of the false teachers as "ungodly men" who pervert God's grace. Tyndale notes that "abusing God's marvelous grace by saying that it allows us to live immoral lives is a constant temptation," which aligns with all commentaries' emphasis on these teachers turning grace into license for sin. All commentators also agree on the three Old Testament examples of judgment: the destroyed Israelites in the wilderness, the fallen angels, and Sodom and Gomorrah, viewing these as warnings against apostasy and ungodliness.

The commentaries converge on the epistle's practical conclusion, with Tyndale emphasizing believers must "devote themselves" to "the doctrinal and ethical core of Christian identity" through building themselves up in faith and prayer. This matches the other commentaries' focus on persevering in truth, though they develop the applications differently.

Points of Difference

The Tyndale commentary takes a more cautious, scholarly approach to contested passages, while the historical commentaries make more definitive theological claims. Regarding the identity of "the Lord" who saved Israel (verse 5), Tyndale notes this refers to "the preexistent Jesus," but presents this as factual without extensive theological elaboration. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown makes the same identification but emphasizes the "perfect oneness" between Jesus and "the God of the Israelite theocracy," while Gill provides detailed exposition on Christ as Mediator.

On the disputed passage about Michael and Moses' body (verse 9), Tyndale simply notes the story "is not in the Old Testament but is preserved in Jewish tradition," taking a neutral stance. Matthew Henry acknowledges interpretive uncertainty, saying "Interpreters are at a loss what is here meant," while Gill confidently interprets it as referring to "the law of Moses" rather than his literal body. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown also favors the literal interpretation but provides extensive detail from apocryphal sources.

The commentaries differ significantly in their approach to predestination in verse 4. Tyndale avoids the topic entirely in its notes on this verse, while Gill extensively defends eternal reprobation, arguing that "reprobation is of the same date with election" and explaining why this "does not make God the author" of sin. Matthew Henry takes a more moderate position, suggesting the phrase could mean "forewritten" in prophecy rather than eternally decreed, noting that "plain Christians had not been troubled with dark, doubtful, and perplexing thoughts about reprobation."

Unique Contributions

Tyndale's unique contribution lies in its accessible, modern study Bible approach that provides essential background without overwhelming readers with theological debates. It offers helpful contextual notes (such as explaining that "faith" in verse 3 refers to "the content of Christian belief" rather than the act of believing) and practical applications (like the three-category interpretation of verses 22-23 for different types of people needing help). Tyndale also uniquely emphasizes manuscript variations, noting textual uncertainties while maintaining pastoral sensitivity.

Jamieson-Fausset-Brown provides unparalleled detailed exegesis, particularly in word studies and cross-referencing. Their unique contribution includes extensive analysis of Greek terms (like explaining "spilades" vs "spiloi" in verse 12) and detailed exploration of Old Testament parallels. They offer the most thorough treatment of the Enoch quotation and provide sophisticated theological connections between Old and New Testament passages.

John Gill uniquely brings extensive rabbinical and Jewish background material, frequently citing Talmudic sources and Jewish traditions that illuminate the text. His commentary provides the most comprehensive treatment of predestination and election, and he alone offers detailed analysis of Jewish interpretations of biblical names and concepts. Gill also provides unique historical context about early Christian practices like the Agape feasts.

Matthew Henry distinctively emphasizes practical Christian living and pastoral applications. His unique contribution lies in connecting every passage to contemporary Christian experience, offering moral reflections and practical warnings. He alone extensively discusses the proper conduct of Christian controversy and the dangers of religious disputes, providing guidance for church relationships that the others largely omit.

Historical & Theological Context

Tyndale's modern evangelical study Bible approach reflects contemporary biblical scholarship's emphasis on accessibility and practical application. Written for lay readers, it avoids contentious theological debates while maintaining conservative evangelical positions. The notes reflect modern textual criticism methods and archaeological insights, presenting information in digestible portions suitable for personal study or small group discussion.

Jamieson-Fausset-Brown's 19th-century Presbyterian context shows in their detailed systematic theology and emphasis on biblical inerrancy. Their commentary reflects the era's confidence in harmonizing apparent contradictions and finding precise theological meanings in every textual detail. Their treatment of predestination and biblical authority reflects Reformed orthodoxy of their period, with extensive use of original languages typical of 19th-century scholarly commentaries.

John Gill's 18th-century Baptist background is evident in his strong Calvinistic theology and detailed defense of particular doctrines like eternal reprobation and perseverance of the saints. His extensive use of rabbinical sources reflects the era's growing interest in Jewish learning, while his polemical tone against "false teachers" mirrors the theological controversies of his day. His Baptist distinctives appear in discussions of church discipline and ordinances.

Matthew Henry's Puritan-influenced approach from the early 18th century emphasizes personal piety and practical godliness over systematic theology. His commentary reflects the Puritan tradition's concern with Christian experience and moral application. His irenic spirit and warnings against religious controversy reflect the period following England's religious civil wars, showing concern for Christian unity while maintaining doctrinal integrity.

Study Questions

Challenge assumptions, probe reasoning, expose tensions

Challenge Assumptions
  1. Jude assumes his readers will accept non-canonical sources like 1 Enoch as authoritative - what does this suggest about our assumptions regarding biblical authority?
  2. If grace truly covers all sin, how can Jude argue that some people 'pervert grace into sensuality' (v4) - isn't grace supposed to be unconditional?
  3. What assumptions about religious tolerance are challenged by Jude's harsh condemnation of false teachers?
  4. Does Jude's certainty about who is 'designated for condemnation' (v4) conflict with beliefs about human free will and divine love?
Probe Reasoning
  1. If Michael the archangel wouldn't pronounce judgment on Satan (v9), what justifies Jude's harsh pronouncements against human false teachers?
  2. How can Jude simultaneously call for mercy toward doubters (v22) while showing no mercy toward the false teachers he condemns?
  3. What is the logical connection between the historical examples in verses 5-7 and the contemporary situation Jude addresses?
  4. Why does Jude focus on sexual immorality when discussing false teaching - what is the underlying connection he's making?
Expose Tensions
  1. How do we reconcile Jude's command to 'contend for the faith' with Jesus' teachings about turning the other cheek and loving enemies?
  2. What tension exists between Jude's predestinarian language ('designated for condemnation' v4) and his call for readers to 'keep yourselves in God's love' (v21)?
  3. If God is 'able to keep you from stumbling' (v24), why does Jude spend most of his letter warning about the danger of false teachers?
  4. How do we balance Jude's exclusivity about 'the faith once delivered' (v3) with the reality of diverse Christian interpretations throughout history?

Observe → Interpret → Apply

Observe
  1. What specific behaviors and attitudes does Jude attribute to the false teachers throughout the epistle?
  2. List all the Old Testament figures and events Jude references - what do you notice about his selection?
  3. What verbs does Jude use to describe what believers should do in verses 20-23?
  4. How does Jude's tone shift from verses 1-4 to verses 5-16 to verses 17-25?
Interpret
  1. What does Jude mean when he says these false teachers 'pervert the grace of our God into sensuality' (v4)?
  2. Why does Jude emphasize that his readers 'once fully knew' these examples of divine judgment (v5)?
  3. What is the significance of Michael's restraint in verse 9 compared to the false teachers' blasphemy in verse 10?
  4. How do the agricultural and celestial metaphors in verses 12-13 help readers understand the nature of false teaching?
Apply
  1. How can believers today 'build themselves up in their most holy faith' as Jude commands in verse 20?
  2. What does it look like practically to 'contend for the faith' (v3) in our current cultural context?
  3. How should Christians apply Jude's three-tiered approach to helping those influenced by false teaching (vv22-23)?
  4. What role should Jude's doxology (vv24-25) play in how we face doctrinal challenges and spiritual warfare?

Six cognitive levels from recall to creation

  2. Name the three Old Testament examples of divine judgment that Jude cites in verses 5-7.
  3. What are the three Old Testament figures mentioned in verse 11 as negative examples?
  4. Identify the four activities believers should engage in according to verses 20-21.
Understand
  1. Explain why Jude changed his writing plan from discussing 'common salvation' to urging readers to 'contend for the faith' (v3).
  2. Summarize the connection between the historical examples in verses 5-7 and the contemporary false teachers in verse 8.
  3. Describe what Jude means by calling false teachers 'waterless clouds' and 'fruitless trees' in verse 12.
Apply
  1. How would you implement Jude's instruction to 'keep yourselves in the love of God' (v21) in daily Christian living?
  2. Demonstrate how a church today might follow Jude's three-fold approach to those affected by false teaching (vv22-23).
  3. Show how believers can 'contend for the faith' (v3) while maintaining Christian love and humility.
Analyze
  1. Compare and contrast Jude's approach to false teachers with Paul's method in his epistles.
  2. Examine the structure of Jude's argument: how do verses 5-16 support his main thesis in verses 3-4?
  3. Break down the literary techniques Jude uses in verses 12-13 - what makes his metaphors so effective?
Evaluate
  1. Assess whether Jude's use of non-canonical sources (like 1 Enoch in vv14-15) strengthens or weakens his argument.
  2. Judge the appropriateness of Jude's harsh language about false teachers for contemporary Christian apologetics.
  3. Critique the balance between Jude's call for theological vigilance and Jesus' commands about judgment and mercy.
Create
  1. Compose a modern version of Jude's nature metaphors (vv12-13) that would resonate with contemporary readers.
  2. Design a church discipleship program based on Jude's four-fold exhortation in verses 20-21.
  3. Develop criteria for distinguishing between the kind of false teaching Jude condemns and legitimate theological diversity.

Academic approach: genre, history, language, structure

Genre & Structure
  1. How does Jude's epistle function as both a letter and a piece of Jewish apocalyptic literature?
  2. Analyze the rhetorical structure of verses 5-11: how does Jude build his argument through historical precedents?
  3. What literary devices does Jude employ in the string of metaphors in verses 12-13, and how do they function together?
Historical Context
  1. What can we infer about the specific false teaching threatening Jude's audience from his emphasis on sexual immorality and rejection of authority?
  2. How does Jude's reference to 'the predictions of the apostles' (v17) situate this letter within early Christian timeline and authority structures?
  3. What does Jude's use of Jewish pseudepigraphical literature (1 Enoch, Assumption of Moses) reveal about his audience's religious background?
  4. How might the phrase 'crept in unawares' (v4) reflect the social dynamics of early Christian communities?
Word Studies
  1. What is the significance of the Greek word 'hapax' (once for all) in verse 3, and how does it relate to Jude's theological argument?
  2. Examine the term 'ungodly' (asebes) - how many times does it appear, and what does this repetition accomplish?
  3. What does the phrase 'glorious ones' (doxas) in verse 8 refer to, and why is blaspheming them significant?
  4. Analyze the compound Greek words Jude creates in verses 12-13 - how do they intensify his descriptions?
Literary Features
  1. How does Jude use the literary pattern of 'then and now' throughout the epistle to make his argument?
  2. What is the function of the inclusio between the opening greeting (vv1-2) and closing doxology (vv24-25)?
  3. Analyze Jude's use of triads throughout the letter - why might this pattern be significant?
  4. How does the shift from third person (about false teachers) to second person (to beloved readers) in verse 17 affect the letter's impact?

Where this fits in God's big story

  2. What does Jude's emphasis on 'the faith once for all delivered to the saints' (v3) reveal about the completion of revelation in Christ?
  3. How does Jude's warning about false teachers fit into the biblical theme of true and false prophets throughout Israel's history?
  4. What role does divine judgment play in Jude's theology, and how does it serve God's redemptive purposes?
Christ Connection
  1. How does Jude's identification of Jesus as 'Master and Lord' (v4) and the one who 'saved a people out of Egypt' (v5) reveal his Christology?
  2. What does 'waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life' (v21) contribute to understanding Christ's role in salvation?
  3. How does the doxology's reference to 'our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord' (v25) illuminate the relationship between Father and Son in redemption?
  4. In what ways does Jude's letter anticipate Christ's final judgment and vindication of the faithful?
God's Character
  1. What aspects of God's character are revealed through Jude's recounting of divine judgment on the disobedient (vv5-7, 11)?
  2. How does the final doxology (vv24-25) summarize God's attributes and their relevance to believers facing false teaching?
  3. What does God's preservation of the angels 'in eternal chains under gloomy darkness' (v6) reveal about divine justice and sovereignty?
  4. How do the opening benediction (v2) and closing doxology (vv24-25) frame our understanding of God's relationship with his people?

Jamieson-Fausset-Brown Bible Commentary

servant of Jesus Christ--as His minister and apostle. brother of James--who was more widely known as bishop of Jerusalem and "brother of the Lord" (that is, either cousin, or stepbrother, being son of Joseph by a former marriage; for ancient traditions universally agree that Mary, Jesus' mother, continued perpetually a virgin). Jude therefore calls himself modestly "brother of James." See my Introduction. to them . . . sanctified by God the Father--The oldest manuscripts and versions, ORIGEN, LUCIFER, and others read, "beloved" for sanctified. If English Version be read, compare Col 1:12; Pe1 1:2. The Greek is not "by," but "in." God the Father's love is the element IN which they are "beloved." Thus the conclusion, Jde 1:21, corresponds, "Keep yourselves in the love of God." Compare "beloved of the Lord" Th2 2:13. preserved in Jesus Christ--"kept." Translate not "in," but as Greek, "FOR Jesus Christ." "Kept continually (so the Greek perfect participle means) by God the Father for Jesus Christ," against the day of His coming. Jude, beforehand, mentions the source and guarantee for the final accomplishment of believers' salvation; lest they should be disheartened by the dreadful evils which he proceeds to announce [BENGEL]. and called--predicated of "them that are beloved in God the Father, and preserved in Jesus Christ: who are called." God's effectual calling in the exercise of His divine prerogative, guarantees their eternal safety.
Verse 14
See Introduction on the source whence Jude derived this prophecy of Enoch. The Holy Spirit, by Jude, has sealed the truth of this much of the matter contained in the book of Enoch, though probably that book, as well as Jude, derived it from tradition (compare Note, see on Jde 1:9). There are reasons given by some for thinking the book of Enoch copied from Jude rather than vice versa. It is striking how, from the first, prophecy hastened towards its consummation. The earliest prophecies of the Redeemer dwell on His second coming in glory, rather than His first coming in lowliness (compare Gen 3:15 with Rom 16:20). Enoch, in his translation without death, illustrated that truth which he all his life preached to the unbelieving world, the certainty of the Lord's coming, and the resurrection of the dead, as the only effectual antidote to their skepticism and self-wise confidence in nature's permanence. And Enoch--Greek, "Moreover, also Enoch," &c. seventh from Adam--Seven is the sacred number. In Enoch, freedom from death and the sacred number are combined: for every seventh object is most highly valued. Jude thus shows the antiquity of the prophecies. Compare Note, see on Jde 1:4, "of old." There were only five fathers between Enoch and Adam. The seventh from Adam prophesied the things which shall close the seventh age of the world [BENGEL]. of these--in relation to these. The reference of his prophecies was not to the antediluvians alone, but to all the ungodly (Jde 1:15). His prophecy applied primarily indeed to the flood, but ultimately to the final judgment. cometh--literally, "came." Prophecy regards the future as certain as if it were past. saints--Holy angels (compare Deu 33:2; Dan 7:10; Zac 14:5; Mat 25:31; Heb 12:22).
Verse 15
This verse and the beginning of Enoch's prophecy is composed in Hebrew poetic parallelism, the oldest specimen extant. Some think Lamech's speech, which is also in poetic parallelism, was composed in mockery of Enoch's prophecy: as Enoch foretold Jehovah's coming to judgment, so Lamech presumes on impunity in polygamy and murder (just as Cain the murderer seemed to escape with impunity). convince--convict. hard speeches--such as are noticed in Jde 1:8, Jde 1:10, Jde 1:16; Mal 3:13-14; contrast Rom 16:17. ungodly sinners--not merely sinners, but proud despisers of God: impious. against him--They who speak against God's children are regarded by God as speaking against Himself.

Verse 14
And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam,.... This was Enoch the son of Jared; his name signifies one "instructed", or "trained up"; as he doubtless was by his father, in the true religion, in the nurture and admonition of the Lord; and was one that had much communion with God; he walked with him, and was translated by him, body and soul, to heaven, and did not see death; Gen 5:18; he is said to be "the seventh from Adam"; not the seventh man from him that was born into the world, for there were no doubt thousands born before him; but he was, as the Jews express it (f), , "the seventh generation" from him; and they have an observation (g), that all sevenths are always beloved by God; the seventh in lands, and the seventh in generations; Adam, Seth, Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared, Enoch, as it is written, Gen 5:24; and this is said partly to distinguish him from others of the same name, and particularly from Enoch the son of Cain, the third: from Adam in his line, as this was the seventh from Adam in the line of Seth; and partly to observe the antiquity of the following prophecy of his: for it is said, he prophesied of these; of these false teachers, and such as they; what would be their sad state and condition at the second coming of Christ to judgment: that he had a spirit of prophecy is evident from the name he gave to his son Methuselah, which signifies, "when he dies is the emission", or the sending out of the waters of the flood, which came to pass the very year he did die. The Arabic writers (h) call him Edris the prophet; and the Jews say (i), that he was in a higher degree than Moses or Elias; they also call (k) him Metatron, the great scribe, a name which they sometimes give to the angel that went before the children of Israel in the wilderness, and which seems to belong to the Messiah: that Enoch wrote a prophecy, and left it behind him in writing, does not appear from hence, or elsewhere; the Jews, in some of their writings, do cite and make mention of the book of Enoch; and there is a fragment now which bears his name, but is a spurious piece, and has nothing like this prophecy in it; wherefore Jude took this not from a book called the "Apocalypse of Enoch", but from tradition; this prophecy being handed down from age to age; and was in full credit with the Jews, and therefore the apostle very appropriately produces it; or rather he had it by divine inspiration, and is as follows: saying, behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousand of his saints; by the "Lord" is meant the Lord Jesus Christ, who is ordained the Judge of quick and dead, and for which he is richly qualified, being omniscient and omnipotent, and faithful and righteous, and who will certainly come again to judge the world in righteousness; for not of his first coming, which was not to judge and condemn, but to seek and save, but of his second coming at the last day is this to be understood; and this is expressed in the present tense, "cometh", in the manner of the prophets, who speak of things future as if they already were, as Isaiah does of the incarnation, sufferings, and death of Christ, and to awaken the attention of persons to it, as if it was near at hand, as also to signify the certainty of it: and when he comes, he will be attended "with ten thousand of his saints": meaning either the souls of glorified saints, even all of them, Th1 3:13, which will come with Christ, and meet the living ones, and be reunited to their own bodies, which will then be raised; or else the holy angels, as in Deu 33:2; and so some copies and the Arabic version read; which will be both for the showing forth of his glory and majesty, and for service in gathering his elect together, as well as for terror to the wicked; and a "behold" is prefixed to all this, to denote the certainty of Christ's coming, and the importance and wonderfulness of it: the ends of his coming follow, (f) Juchasin, fol. 5. 2. Verse 15
To execute judgment upon all,.... Quick and dead, small and great, high and low, rich and poor, good and bad, righteous and wicked, sheep and goats; to pass the definitive sentence on each, that of absolution, life, and happiness, on his own people, and that of condemnation, death, and misery, on the wicked; which will be done in the most strict and righteous manner: and to convince all that are ungodly among them; those who are without God, the fear of him love to him, or faith in him; who have lived without the worship of him, or in a false worship; and particularly false teachers are here meant, the same as in Jde 1:4; who will then be convicted in their own consciences, by that clear evidence, and full light, in which things will be set: of all their ungodly deeds; both against law and Gospel: which they have ungodly committed; which they lived in the commission of, and continually practised in a vile manner, publicly, and in defiance of heaven, and with seared consciences: and of all their hard speeches, which ungodly sinners have spoken against him; either "against" God, as the Vulgate Latin version reads, against his being, his perfections, his providence, his purposes, his word, and worship; or rather against Jesus Christ the Lord, who will come to judge them, against his person and offices, his blood, righteousness, and sacrifice; his ministers and people, his truths and ordinances.
Matthew Henry's Complete Commentary
Matthew Henry
We have here, I. An account of the penman of this epistle, a character of the church, the blessings and privileges of that happy society (Jde 1:1, Jde 1:2). II. The occasion of writing this epistle (Jde 1:3). III. A character of evil and perverse men, who had already sprung up in that infant state of the church, and would be succeeded by others of the like evil spirit and temper in after-times (Jde 1:4). IV. A caution against hearkening to and following after such, from the severity of God towards the unbelieving murmuring Israelites at their coming out of Egypt, the angels that fell, the sin and punishment of Sodom and Gomorrah (Jde 1:5-7). V. To these the apostle likens the seducers against whom he was warning them, and describes them at large, (Jde 1:8-10, inclusive). VI. Then (as specially suitable to his argument) he cites an ancient prophecy of Enoch foretelling and describing the future judgment (Jde 1:14, Jde 1:15). VII. He enlarges on the seducers' character, and guards against the offence which honest minds might be apt to take at the so early permission of such things, by showing that it was foretold long before that so it must be (Jde 1:16-19). VIII. Exhorts them to perseverance in the faith, fervency in prayer, watchfulness against falling from the love of God, and a lively hope of eternal life (Jde 1:20, Jde 1:21). IX. Directs them how to act towards the erroneous and scandalous (Jde 1:22, Jde 1:23). And, X. Closes with an admirable doxology in the last two verses.
