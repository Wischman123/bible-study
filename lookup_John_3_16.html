Commentary Comparison

Tyndale Open Study Notes · Jamieson-Fausset-Brown Bible Commentary · John Gill's Exposition of the Whole Bible · Matthew Henry's Complete Commentary

Points of Agreement

Tyndale establishes the foundational agreement that spiritual rebirth is absolutely essential for entering God's kingdom, requiring divine intervention rather than human effort. All commentators unite on this core necessity of regeneration.

The commentaries converge on several key interpretive points: Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus represents a pivotal teaching about salvation that transcends human understanding. Gill, Henry, and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown all echo Tyndale's emphasis that natural human reasoning cannot grasp spiritual truths without God's illuminating work.

All four sources agree that the "born again" language refers to a complete spiritual transformation from above, not merely moral improvement. They consistently interpret the wind metaphor as illustrating the Spirit's sovereign, mysterious, yet powerful work in regeneration. The bronze serpent typology is unanimously understood as prefiguring Christ's crucifixion for humanity's salvation.

Finally, following Tyndale's lead, all commentators affirm John the Baptist's humble recognition of his subordinate role to Christ, understanding the bridegroom imagery as John's acknowledgment of Christ's rightful preeminence and the church's belonging to Him.

Points of Difference

Tyndale approaches the text with modern evangelical clarity, focusing on practical application and accessible explanation of spiritual rebirth, while the historical commentaries engage in more detailed exegetical analysis and theological controversy.

The most significant difference emerges in their treatment of baptism in verse 5. Tyndale emphasizes the spiritual reality that John the Baptist baptized with water while Jesus baptizes with the Spirit. In contrast, Henry suggests some connection to the ordinance of baptism, while Gill explicitly argues against baptismal regeneration, and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown sees water as symbolizing spiritual purification by the Holy Spirit.

Their handling of theological complexity varies markedly. Tyndale provides straightforward explanations suitable for general Bible study, while Gill extensively quotes Jewish sources and rabbinic parallels. Henry offers detailed verse-by-verse exposition with moral application, and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown provides rich theological reflection but with more academic density.

Regarding the scope of God's love in verse 16, Tyndale affirms God's love extends beyond racial and national limits to all who oppose Him, while Gill argues specifically that "the world" refers primarily to the Gentiles in distinction from the Jews, providing extensive Jewish sources to support his interpretation.

Unique Contributions

Tyndale's distinctive contribution lies in its modern study Bible approach, providing clear cross-references, thematic connections, and practical applications. It uniquely emphasizes the symbolic significance of night representing the realm of evil and unbelief, and offers helpful clarifications about textual uncertainties (such as where Jesus' words end and John's commentary begins in verses 16-21).

Jamieson-Fausset-Brown brings exceptional literary and theological sensitivity, offering beautiful reflections on John 3:16 as history's most influential Gospel proclamation. Their analysis of Christ's "lifting up" uniquely traces this theme through John's Gospel, and they provide profound insights into the bronze serpent typology that the others don't match.

Gill contributes unparalleled Jewish historical context, extensively quoting rabbinical sources to illuminate the background of Nicodemus and Jewish customs. His detailed identification of the historical Nicodemus with "Nicodemon ben Gorion" and his argument about the meaning of "the world" in verse 16 provide unique historical perspective.

Henry offers the most comprehensive pastoral application, systematically addressing practical implications for Christian living. His treatment of Nicodemus coming "by night" explores multiple interpretive possibilities (prudence, zeal, or cowardice) that others don't consider, and he provides extensive moral exhortations throughout.

Historical & Theological Context

Tyndale represents contemporary evangelical scholarship designed for accessible Bible study, emphasizing clarity and practical application for modern readers. Its approach reflects current evangelical priorities: clear Gospel presentation, emphasis on personal relationship with God, and concern for practical Christian living.

The historical commentaries reflect their distinct eras and theological environments. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (19th century) demonstrates Reformed theological precision with literary sensitivity, characteristic of Scottish Presbyterian scholarship. Their approach reflects careful exegetical method combined with devotional warmth.

Gill (18th century) represents Baptist theological concerns, particularly defending believer's baptism against infant baptism and emphasizing regeneration preceding rather than following water baptism. His extensive use of Jewish sources reflects the scholarly resources available to his era and his particular interest in defending Baptist distinctives.

Henry (17th-18th century) embodies Puritan expository preaching, focusing on practical godliness and moral application. His comprehensive verse-by-verse treatment reflects the Puritan emphasis on thorough biblical exposition combined with pastoral care for souls.

While Tyndale serves modern students seeking accessible biblical understanding, the historical commentaries preserve the theological depth and controversy of their respective periods, offering rich exegetical tradition that informs but differs from contemporary evangelical study Bible approaches.

Study Questions

Challenge assumptions, probe reasoning, expose tensions

Challenge Assumptions
  1. Nicodemus assumes his religious credentials and recognition of Jesus as a teacher are sufficient—what does Jesus's response suggest about the adequacy of human religious achievement?
  2. The disciples assume that Jesus's growing popularity threatens John's ministry—what assumption about success and competition does John's response challenge?
  3. What assumptions about the nature of God's judgment does verse 19 challenge by locating condemnation in human choice rather than divine decree?
Probe Reasoning
  1. If someone 'cannot see the kingdom of God' without being born again (v.3), what does this imply about human spiritual perception and free will?
  2. How can people be 'condemned already' (v.18) if God's purpose is salvation rather than condemnation (v.17)?
  3. If the Spirit, like wind, moves where it wishes (v.8), what does this suggest about human control over spiritual transformation?
Expose Tensions
  1. Is there a contradiction between Jesus's inclusive statement 'whoever believes' (v.16) and his exclusive claim that only those born again can see God's kingdom?
  2. How do we reconcile God's universal love for 'the world' (v.16) with the reality that many 'love darkness rather than light' (v.19)?
  3. Can the concepts of divine sovereignty in spiritual birth and human responsibility for belief be held in tension, or must we choose one?

Observe → Interpret → Apply

Observe
  1. What specific details does John provide about Nicodemus's identity and approach to Jesus in verses 1-2?
  2. How does the metaphor of wind/Spirit in verse 8 connect to the concept of spiritual birth?
  3. What progression do you see in John the Baptist's three statements about his relationship to Jesus in verses 28-30?
  4. Which key terms or phrases are repeated throughout this chapter, and where do they appear?
Interpret
  1. What does Jesus mean by the distinction between 'earthly things' and 'heavenly things' in verse 12?
  2. How does the phrase 'born again' (v.3) relate to 'born from above' as alternate translations of the same Greek word?
  3. What is the significance of Jesus identifying himself as 'he who descended from heaven' in verse 13?
  4. How does John the Baptist's bridegroom metaphor (v.29) illuminate the nature of his ministry?
Apply
  1. What religious or intellectual assumptions in your own life might need to be challenged like Nicodemus's were?
  2. How can John the Baptist's joy in decreasing while Christ increases inform your approach to ministry or service?
  3. In what ways do people today 'love the darkness rather than the light' (v.19), and how can believers respond?
  4. What would it look like to 'come to the light' (v.21) in specific areas of your current circumstances?

Six cognitive levels from recall to creation

Remember
  1. What are the specific titles given to Nicodemus in verse 1?
  2. According to verse 16, what motivated God to give his only Son?
  3. Where was John baptizing according to verse 23, and why did he choose that location?
Understand
  1. Explain the connection Jesus makes between the bronze serpent incident and his own mission in verses 14-15.
  2. What does Jesus mean when he says 'that which is born of the flesh is flesh' in verse 6?
  3. Summarize John the Baptist's explanation of why Jesus's growing popularity doesn't threaten him (vv.27-30).
Apply
  1. How would you explain the concept of being 'born again' to someone who, like Nicodemus, thinks only in physical terms?
  2. In what situations today might believers need to decrease so that Christ might increase?
  3. How can the principle that 'whoever does what is true comes to the light' (v.21) guide ethical decision-making?
Analyze
  1. Compare and contrast Nicodemus's approach to Jesus with John the Baptist's disciples' concerns about Jesus in verses 25-26.
  2. Break down the logical progression in Jesus's argument from verses 11-13 about testimony and authority.
  3. Examine how the themes of light/darkness and belief/unbelief interconnect in verses 18-21.
Evaluate
  1. Assess whether Nicodemus's nighttime visit demonstrates cowardice, wisdom, or something else entirely.
  2. Judge the effectiveness of Jesus's teaching method with Nicodemus—was the confusion productive or counterproductive?
  3. Critique the claim that 'no one receives his testimony' (v.32) in light of the growing number of disciples mentioned.
Create
  1. Design a modern parable that would convey the concept of spiritual rebirth to a contemporary skeptic.
  2. Compose a prayer that reflects John the Baptist's heart attitude in verses 27-30.
  3. Develop a teaching outline showing how John 3 demonstrates the necessity, nature, and results of spiritual transformation.

Academic approach: genre, history, language, structure

Genre & Structure
  1. How does John structure the dialogue between Jesus and Nicodemus to show escalating revelation and resistance?
  2. What literary function does the transition in verse 22 serve between the Nicodemus encounter and the Baptist's testimony?
  3. How does the narrator's voice in verses 16-21 relate to Jesus's direct speech—is this still Jesus speaking or John's commentary?
Historical Context
  1. What would Nicodemus's position as 'a ruler of the Jews' have entailed in terms of Sanhedrin membership and religious authority?
  2. How do the competing baptismal ministries described in verses 22-26 reflect the historical transition from John's to Jesus's ministry?
  3. What significance would 'teacher of Israel' (v.10) have carried in first-century Jewish religious hierarchy?
Word Studies
  1. How does the double meaning of Greek 'anothen' (again/from above) in verse 3 create intentional ambiguity in Jesus's statement?
  2. What is the significance of the Greek 'pneuma' appearing as both 'wind' and 'Spirit' in verse 8?
  3. How does the Greek construction of 'believes in him' (pisteuon eis auton) in verse 16 differ from simple intellectual assent?
Literary Features
  1. What irony does John create by having 'the teacher of Israel' unable to understand spiritual birth?
  2. How do the metaphors of birth, wind, light/darkness, and bridegroom work together to convey John's theological themes?
  3. What is the effect of Jesus's 'truly, truly' (amen, amen) formula in verses 3, 5, and 11 on the dialogue's tone?

Where this fits in God's big story

Salvation Story
  1. How does the bronze serpent typology in verses 14-15 connect Jesus's crucifixion to God's pattern of salvation through judgment?
  2. What does the necessity of being 'born again' reveal about humanity's condition since the Fall?
  3. How do verses 16-18 encapsulate the gospel message within the broader narrative of God's redemptive plan?
Christ Connection
  1. How does Jesus's claim to be the one who 'descended from heaven' (v.13) establish his unique role as mediator?
  2. In what ways does the 'lifting up' of the Son of Man (v.14) encompass both crucifixion and exaltation?
  3. How does Jesus's role as the bridegroom (implied in v.29) connect to Old Testament imagery of God's relationship with Israel?
God's Character
  1. What does verse 16's declaration that 'God so loved the world' reveal about divine motivation in redemption?
  2. How does God's desire to save rather than condemn (v.17) reflect his character throughout Scripture?
  3. What does the giving of the Spirit 'without measure' (v.34) demonstrate about God's generosity in the new covenant?

Scripture → Observation → Application → Prayer

Scripture
  1. Which verse or passage in John 3 speaks most directly to your current spiritual condition?
  2. What phrase from Jesus's dialogue with Nicodemus captures your attention and why?
Observation
  1. What do you observe about the different ways people respond to Jesus's message in this chapter?
  2. What does this passage reveal about the nature of spiritual transformation that you hadn't noticed before?
  3. How does John the Baptist's attitude toward Jesus's increasing ministry challenge typical human responses to being overshadowed?
Application
  1. In what area of your life do you need to move from intellectual understanding to spiritual transformation like Nicodemus?
  2. How can you cultivate John the Baptist's joy in seeing Christ increase even if it means you must decrease?
  3. What specific step can you take this week to 'come to the light' rather than hiding in darkness?
Prayer
  1. How would you pray for someone you know who, like Nicodemus, is spiritually curious but not yet transformed?
  2. What would you ask God for regarding your own willingness to decrease so that Christ might increase in your life?
  3. How might you pray in response to the truth that God's love motivated the gift of his Son?

A balanced blend drawing from multiple study methods

Observe
  1. What patterns do you notice in how Jesus responds to Nicodemus's three questions throughout verses 3-10?
  2. How many times does the phrase 'truly, truly' appear, and what follows each occurrence?
  3. What contrasts does John establish between Nicodemus and John the Baptist in their respective responses to Jesus?
Context
  1. Given that Nicodemus was 'a ruler of the Jews' (v.1), what would his religious expectations have been regarding how one enters God's kingdom?
  2. How does the bronze serpent reference (v.14) from Numbers 21 illuminate Jesus's teaching about being 'lifted up'?
Interpret
  1. What is the relationship between being 'born of water and the Spirit' (v.5) and the two baptisms mentioned later in verses 22-23?
  2. How does Jesus's statement 'you do not receive our testimony' (v.11) shift from singular to plural, and what does this suggest?
Connect
  1. How does John 3:16's declaration of God's love connect to the light/darkness imagery that follows in verses 19-21?
  2. What parallels exist between Nicodemus coming 'by night' (v.2) and the broader theme of light versus darkness in John's Gospel?
Evaluate
  1. Is there tension between God not sending his Son 'to condemn the world' (v.17) and people being 'condemned already' (v.18)?
  2. How do you reconcile Jesus's harsh tone with Nicodemus ('Are you the teacher of Israel...?' v.10) with his mission of love described in verse 16?
Reflect
  1. When have you, like Nicodemus, approached spiritual truth with purely intellectual categories that needed to be transformed?
  2. How might John the Baptist's attitude 'He must increase, but I must decrease' (v.30) challenge contemporary Christian leadership models?

Jamieson-Fausset-Brown Bible Commentary
Robert Jamieson, A.R. Fausset, David Brown
NIGHT INTERVIEW OF NICODEMUS WITH JESUS. (John 3:1-21) Nicodemus--In this member of the Sanhedrim sincerity and timidity are seen struggling together.
Verse 16
For God so loved, &c.--What proclamation of the Gospel has been so oft on the lips of missionaries and preachers in every age since it was first uttered? What has sent such thrilling sensations through millions of mankind? What has been honored to bring such multitudes to the feet of Christ? What to kindle in the cold and selfish breasts of mortals the fires of self-sacrificing love to mankind, as these words of transparent simplicity, yet overpowering majesty? The picture embraces several distinct compartments: "THE WORLD"--in its widest sense--ready "to perish"; the immense "LOVE OF GOD" to that perishing world, measurable only, and conceivable only, by the gift which it drew forth from Him; THE GIFT itself--"He so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son," or, in the language of Paul, "spared not His own Son" (Rom 8:32), or in that addressed to Abraham when ready to offer Isaac on the altar, "withheld not His Son, His only Son, whom He loved" (Gen 22:16); the FRUIT of this stupendous gift--not only deliverance from impending "perdition," but the bestowal of everlasting life; the MODE in which all takes effect--by "believing" on the Son. How would Nicodemus' narrow Judaism become invisible in the blaze of this Sun of righteousness seen rising on "the world" with healing in His wings! (Mal 4:2).

John Gill's Exposition of the Whole Bible
John Gill
For John was not yet cast into prison. As he afterwards was by Herod, for the sake of Herodias, because he reproved Herod for taking her to be his wife, when she was wife to his brother Philip; see Mat 14:3; and this circumstance shows, that these things were done before that journey of Christ into Galilee, mentioned in Mat 4:12. . John 3:25 joh 3:25 joh 3:25 joh 3:25Then there arose a question,.... A dispute, or controversy, occasioned by the baptism, of John and Christ: between some of John's disciples, and the Jews. The Syriac and Persic versions read, "between one of John's disciples, and a certain Jew"; and Nonnus renders it, "with an Hebrew man"; and so the Alexandrian manuscript; many others read, "with a Jew": the contention between them was about purifying; either about the ceremonial purifications, and ablutions commanded in the law of Moses; or concerning the various washings of persons, and vessels, according to the traditions of the elders, which the Jews in common were very tenacious of; and which they thought were brought into neglect, and contempt, by the baptism of John: and this seems to have been occasioned by the baptism of Christ; which the Jew might improve against the disciple of John, and urge, that since another, besides his master, had set up baptizing, who could tell which was most right and safest to follow? and therefore it would have been much better, if no such rite at all had been used by any, but that the purifications required by the law of Moses, and by their elders, had been strictly and solely attended to.
Verse 16
For God sent not his Son into the world,.... God did send his Son into the world in the likeness of sinful flesh, being made of a woman, and made under the law; and which is an instance of his great love, and not of any disrespect to his Son, or of any inequality between them: but then this was not to condemn the world; even any part of it, or any in it: not the Gentiles, as the Jews thought he would; for though God had suffered them to walk in their own ways, and had winked at, or overlooked the times of their ignorance, and had sent no prophet unto them, nor made any revelation of his will, or any discovery of his special grace unto them; yet he sent his Son now, not to destroy them for their idolatry, and wickedness, but to be the Saviour of them: nor the Jews; for as impenitent and unbelieving, and as wicked as they were, he did not accuse them to the Father, nor judge and condemn them; he was to come again in power and great glory, when he would take vengeance on them, and cause wrath to come upon them to the uttermost, for their disbelief and rejection of him; but this was not his business now: nor the wicked of the world in general; to judge, and condemn them, will be his work, when he comes a second time, in the day God has appointed to judge the world in righteousness. But the end of his mission, and first coming is, that the world through him might be saved; even the world of the elect in general, whom God determined to save, and has chosen, to obtain salvation by Jesus Christ, and has appointed Christ to be the salvation of; and who being sent, came into the world to seek and save them; and his chosen people among the Gentiles in particular: wherefore he is said to be God's salvation to the ends of the earth: and all the ends of the earth are called upon to look unto him, and be saved by him, Isa 49:6.
